Why the ISS rules do not carry over

Three things change when a fire leaves low Earth orbit. Each is backed by records you can open.

Gravity decides how a flame feeds

In orbit there is no buoyancy, so the first response to a fire is to turn off the fans. With some gravity, hot gas rises and pulls fresh air in after it. NASA Glenn states that stopping ventilation is not an effective way to put out a fire in lunar or Martian gravity.

See the airflow rule on the Moon

Smoke and dust look alike

On the station the detectors missed some smoke from common wire and insulation plastics. On the Moon, chamber tests with lunar regolith simulant show smoke and dust overlapping in particle size and setting off the detector.

See the false-alarm rule in the rover

The cabin is a different place

NASA states that the lander, lunar habitat and pressurized rover nominally use 8.2 psia with 34% oxygen. Small volumes turn a rescue tool into a hazard: a CO₂ extinguisher discharged in a small cabin can exceed safe limits.

    How to use it

    1. 1. Pick a cabin. Five presets from agency sources, or build your own on the audit page.
    2. 2. Read the verdicts. Each of ten ISS-era rules is marked as holding, weakening, breaking or unknown for that cabin.
    3. 3. Check the evidence. Every verdict lists the experiments and sources it rests on, with the place in each source. Browse them all.

    Verdicts are rules, not model output

    The verdict engine is plain deterministic code, so the same cabin always gives the same answer. An optional SI layer can explain search results from the records, and is off unless a service is configured. Where SI fits.
